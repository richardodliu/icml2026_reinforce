\section{Practical Considerations} \label{appendix:best}

\subsection{Sampling and Reward Design}

The two variants differ in how they allocate a response budget and construct the reward baseline. With $B$ responses per rollout batch, single-response sampling can cover $B$ prompts, whereas sampling $k$ responses per prompt covers $B/k$ prompts. The latter allocation provides within-prompt reward comparisons for the baseline variant.

For multi-turn tool use, group-mean centering compares each response with alternatives to the same prompt. A group with identical rewards has zero centered task reward before batch normalization, while a mixed-outcome group retains positive and negative reward differences. All responses remain in the training batch after centering. Subtracting the group mean also removes any reward offset common to the group. For example, changing binary rewards from $0/1$ to $-1/1$ multiplies the centered rewards by two; subsequent global standardization cancels this factor when the numerical stabilizer is omitted. With token-weighted global moments, the batch mean of the centered rewards can be nonzero when response lengths differ.

For general-domain RLHF, the $k=1$ configuration allocates the rollout budget to broader prompt coverage. Section~4.1 reports competitive preference-alignment scores and shorter responses than GRPO under this configuration. For tasks with multiple sampled responses, the baseline variant provides prompt-specific comparisons together with a scale shared across the batch. Reward design, response length, and the relative weight of KL regularization jointly determine the resulting training signal.

\subsection{Evidence from Related Systems}
Several subsequent studies examine global normalization in reasoning systems. Their experiments provide comparisons across reward schemes, model scales, and output-length constraints.

\paragraph{LitePPO}~\citep{liu2025part} combines group-mean centering, batch-level standard deviation, and token-level loss aggregation. Its normalization experiments favor the global standard deviation when paired with a group-level mean, while comparisons of full batch normalization vary with the reward scheme. These results support treating reward centering and scale estimation as separate design choices.

\paragraph{ScaleRL}~\citep{khatri2025art} compares prompt-level, batch-level, and no variance normalization, reporting similar performance with a marginal advantage for batch-level normalization. Its larger-scale leave-one-out study includes runs with a 16,000 GPU-hour budget. ScaleRL uses batch-level normalization in its final training configuration.

\paragraph{DLER}~\citep{liu2025dler} evaluates normalization under output truncation. In its DeepSeek-R1-7B experiment, batch-wise normalization preserves AIME-24 accuracy as response length decreases, whereas group-wise normalization shows declining accuracy. The reported difference occurs during training with reduced output lengths.
