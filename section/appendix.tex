\section{Response-wise loss reduction and implementation}\label{appendix:batch_mean}
\textbf{The objective in \Cref{eq:ppo} uses a mean over valid tokens within each response, followed by a mean over responses in the batch.} Response averaging assigns equal weight to each sampled response.

Let $m_{i,t}\in\{0,1\}$ indicate a valid response token included in the policy loss, and let $n_i=\sum_t m_{i,t}>0$. For $B$ responses, the policy loss is
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{
 L_{\mathrm{PG}}(\theta)=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{i=1}^{B}
 \left[\frac{1}{n_i}\sum_t m_{i,t}\,\ell_{i,t}(\theta)\right],
 \qquad
 \ell_{i,t}(\theta)=-f_{i,t}(\theta,\operatorname{sg}[\widehat A_{i,t}]).
 }
 \label{eq:batch_mean}
\end{equation}
Here $\widehat A$ denotes $A^{\mathrm{norm}}$,  $f$ is the clipped surrogate in \Cref{eq:ppo}, and $\operatorname{sg}$ denotes stop-gradient. Padding and positions outside the response loss mask do not enter the numerator or token count. The separate $k_2$ term in \cref{eq:baseline_loss} uses the same reduction.

\paragraph{Response weighting.}
Each response has outer weight $1/B$, and each valid token within response $i$ has weight $1/(Bn_i)$. This differs from dividing the sum of all token losses by the total number of valid tokens:
\begin{equation}
 L_{\mathrm{token}}=\frac{\sum_{i,t}m_{i,t}\ell_{i,t}}{\sum_i n_i}.
\end{equation}
The latter weights each response in proportion to its length. The two reductions agree when all responses have equal length. With a fixed number $k$ of responses per prompt, batch averaging is also equivalent to averaging the per-response losses within each prompt and then across prompts.

\paragraph{Minibatches and accumulation.}
For a minibatch $\mathcal M$, replace $B$ by $|\mathcal M|$ in \cref{eq:batch_mean}. To recover the same mean when combining uneven microbatches or distributed shards, their loss sums must be weighted by their response counts. The normalization moments are associated with the rollout batch; changing the partition into minibatches does not change the definition of the already computed advantages.

\paragraph{Terminal reward and token returns.}
For the basic variant, write $d_{i,t}=\log(\pi_{\mathrm{old}}/\pi_{ref})$ at the sampled token. The immediate reward can be written as
\begin{equation}
 \widetilde r_{i,t}=\mathbf{1}\{t=T_i\}r(q_i,o_i)-\beta d_{i,t}.
\end{equation}
With unit discount, its return is $\sum_{u=t}^{T_i}\widetilde r_{i,u}=A_{q_i,o_{i,t}}$ from \Cref{eq:adv}. Thus, a terminal task reward contributes to the return at every preceding response position. For \varn, the group-centered terminal reward in \cref{eq:group_center} is broadcast to response positions before global normalization.


\section{Finite-sample properties of normalization}\label{appendix:normalization}\label{appendix:proof_GRPO}
We study rewards standardized by the mean and variance of the same sample. Let
\begin{equation}
 r_i=\mu+\epsilon_i,\qquad \epsilon_i\overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2),\qquad \sigma>0,
\end{equation}
and define $\bar\epsilon=N^{-1}\sum_i\epsilon_i$,
$D_N^2=N^{-1}\sum_i(\epsilon_i-\bar\epsilon)^2$, and
$Z_{i,N}=(\epsilon_i-\bar\epsilon)/D_N$ for $N\geq2$. The population-standardized target is $\epsilon_i/\sigma$. We omit the numerical stabilizer for this analysis; $D_N>0$ almost surely under the stated model.

\begin{proposition}[Finite-sample bound]\label{prop:bound}
For any nonconstant sample of size $N\geq2$, $|Z_{i,N}|\leq\sqrt{N-1}$.
Consequently, under the Gaussian model, $\mathbb{E}[Z_{i,N}\mid\epsilon_i]$ cannot equal $\epsilon_i/\sigma$ almost surely for finite $N$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Set $x_j=\epsilon_j-\bar\epsilon$, so $\sum_jx_j=0$. By Cauchy--Schwarz,
\begin{equation}
 x_i^2=\left(\sum_{j\ne i}x_j\right)^2
 \leq(N-1)\sum_{j\ne i}x_j^2.
\end{equation}
Therefore $\sum_jx_j^2\geq Nx_i^2/(N-1)$, giving
$Z_{i,N}^2=Nx_i^2/\sum_jx_j^2\leq N-1$.
The conditional expectation obeys the same bound, whereas
$|\epsilon_i|/\sigma>\sqrt{N-1}$ has positive probability for a Gaussian variable. Equality with the standardized target cannot hold almost surely.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Conditional moments of the numerator and denominator.}
The centered numerator satisfies
\begin{equation}
 \mathbb{E}[\epsilon_i-\bar\epsilon\mid\epsilon_i]
 =\left(1-\frac1N\right)\epsilon_i.
\end{equation}
Using $D_N^2=N^{-1}\sum_j\epsilon_j^2-\bar\epsilon^2$ and independence,
\begin{align}
 \mathbb{E}[\bar\epsilon^2\mid\epsilon_i]
 &=\frac{\epsilon_i^2+(N-1)\sigma^2}{N^2},\\
 \mathbb{E}[D_N^2\mid\epsilon_i]
 &=\frac{(N-1)^2}{N^2}\sigma^2+\frac{N-1}{N^2}\epsilon_i^2.
 \label{eq:condD2}
\end{align}
These identities describe how the included observation affects the normalization statistics. Proposition~\ref{prop:bound} establishes the finite-sample discrepancy through a direct bound on the ratio.

\paragraph{An exact two-response example.}
For $N=2$, $D_2=|\epsilon_1-\epsilon_2|/2$ and
$Z_{1,2}=\operatorname{sign}(\epsilon_1-\epsilon_2)$ almost surely. Thus,
\begin{equation}
 \mathbb{E}[Z_{1,2}\mid\epsilon_1=x]=2\Phi(x/\sigma)-1,
 \label{eq:two_sample}
\end{equation}
where $\Phi$ is the standard normal cumulative distribution function. This bounded, nonlinear function differs from the linear population-standardized value $x/\sigma$. It illustrates how a small group changes the reward scale through self-normalization.

\begin{proposition}[Large-sample limit]\label{prop:limit}
Under the Gaussian model, for any fixed $i$, $Z_{i,N}\to\epsilon_i/\sigma$ almost surely and in $L^1$ as $N\to\infty$. In particular,
\begin{equation}
 \mathbb{E}\left[\left|\mathbb{E}[Z_{i,N}\mid\epsilon_i]-\epsilon_i/\sigma\right|\right]\longrightarrow0.
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The strong law gives $\bar\epsilon\to0$ and $D_N^2\to\sigma^2$ almost surely, establishing the first limit. By exchangeability and $\sum_{j=1}^{N}Z_{j,N}^2=N$, we have $\mathbb{E}[Z_{i,N}^2]=1$ for every $N$. This uniform second-moment bound implies uniform integrability, so the almost-sure convergence also holds in $L^1$. Conditional Jensen's inequality gives
\begin{equation}
 \mathbb{E}\left[\left|\mathbb{E}[Z_{i,N}-\epsilon_i/\sigma\mid\epsilon_i]\right|\right]
 \leq\mathbb{E}\left[|Z_{i,N}-\epsilon_i/\sigma|\right]\to0.
\end{equation}
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Numerical stabilizer.}
With a fixed stabilizer $\eta>0$, $(\epsilon_i-\bar\epsilon)/(D_N+\eta)$ converges instead to $\epsilon_i/(\sigma+\eta)$. The unstabilized population-standardization limit applies when the stabilizer is omitted or tends to zero. The finite-sample bound remains valid with a nonnegative stabilizer for nonconstant samples; constant samples have zero numerator and return zero when the stabilizer is positive.

\paragraph{Implications for global normalization.}
The propositions isolate the effect of normalization sample size: estimated moments approach their population values as the number of independent observations grows. A shared batch scale can use observations across many prompt groups, rather than estimating a separate scale from each small group. For grouped or token-level data, the effective sample size depends on the number of independent trajectories and their dependence structure.

\paragraph{Relationship to baselines and gradients.}
For an unscaled group mean, $\mathbb{E}[r_i-\bar r_q\mid r_i,q]=(1-1/k)(r_i-\mu_q)$ when responses are conditionally independent with mean reward $\mu_q$. This is the same factor appearing in \Cref{eq:rloo_relation}. The propositions describe convergence to a standardized scalar target. Policy-gradient expectations additionally involve the joint distribution of these normalized rewards and the policy score function.


\section{KL regularization as a gradient surrogate}\label{appendix:kl}\label{appendix:kl_design}
For a fixed context, write $p_\theta(y)=\pi_\theta(y\mid h)$, $q(y)=\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid h)$, and $z(y)=\log[p_\theta(y)/q(y)]$. Assume common support and sufficient regularity to interchange differentiation and expectation. The reverse KL is
\begin{equation}
 D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_\theta\|q)=\mathbb{E}_{y\sim p_\theta}[z(y)],
 \qquad
 \nabla_\theta D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_\theta\|q)
 =\mathbb{E}_{p_\theta}[z(y)\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(y)],
 \label{eq:rkl_gradient}
\end{equation}
using $\mathbb{E}_{p_\theta}[\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta]=0$.

\paragraph{Value estimators and differentiated losses.}
The common expressions are
\begin{equation}
 k_1(z)=z,\qquad k_2(z)=\tfrac12 z^2,\qquad k_3(z)=e^{-z}-1+z.
\end{equation}
Both $k_1$ and $k_3$ have expectation $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_\theta\|q)$ under on-policy sampling, since $\mathbb{E}_{p_\theta}[e^{-z}]=1$. When sampled actions are treated as fixed during automatic differentiation, their pointwise gradients differ:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
 \nabla_\theta k_1 &=\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta,\\
 \nabla_\theta k_2 &=z\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta,\\
 \nabla_\theta k_3 &=(1-q/p_\theta)\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:kl_pointwise}
\end{equation}
The on-policy expectation of the $k_2$ pointwise gradient is exactly \cref{eq:rkl_gradient}. This identity motivates the separate KL term in \varn~\citep{liu2025rethinking}. It is distinct from differentiating the entire expectation $\mathbb{E}_{p_\theta}[k_2]$, which also differentiates the sampling distribution.

Under the same fixed-context assumptions, the expected pointwise gradient of $k_1$ is zero. The $k_1$ expression can instead enter the reward and act as a detached score-function coefficient, as in the returns used by the basic variant. The expected pointwise gradient of $k_3$ equals $-\mathbb{E}_q[\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta]$, the forward-KL gradient. Thus, its on-policy mean estimates reverse KL, while its mean pointwise gradient follows forward KL.

\paragraph{Numerical sensitivity.}
The $k_3$ pointwise gradient contains the importance ratio $q/p_\theta$, which can become large when the current policy assigns low probability to actions favored by the reference. Computing this ratio as $\exp(-z)$ can also overflow for sufficiently negative $z$. The $k_2$ surrogate uses the log ratio in place of this exponential ratio. Its gradient magnitude and variance depend on the joint distribution of the log ratio and the score function.

\paragraph{Rollout reuse.}
The on-policy identities apply when the sampled actions follow the current policy. For actions sampled from $p_{\mathrm{old}}$, a fixed-context importance-weighted gradient satisfies
\begin{equation}
 \mathbb{E}_{p_{\mathrm{old}}}\left[
 \frac{p_\theta(y)}{p_{\mathrm{old}}(y)}z(y)\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(y)
 \right]=\nabla_\theta D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_\theta\|q).
\end{equation}
The practical separate loss in \cref{eq:baseline_loss} is the unweighted $k_2$ surrogate on the rollout data. Its expected pointwise gradient equals the reverse-KL gradient at the on-policy point and changes as the policy moves away from the rollout policy. For autoregressive policies, the fixed-context statement concerns the conditional next-token distribution.


\section{Design implications and sampling budgets}\label{appendix:design}
\paragraph{Local baseline and global scale.}
For a prompt with $k>1$ responses, let $C_{q,i}=r_i-k^{-1}\sum_j r_j$. Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rloo_relation}
 C_{q,i}=\frac{k-1}{k}\left(r_i-\frac{1}{k-1}\sum_{j\ne i}r_j\right)
 =\frac{k-1}{k}A_i^{\mathrm{RLOO}}.
\end{equation}
The group baseline compares a response with alternatives to the same prompt; global scaling supplies a shared scale across prompts. Homogeneous groups have zero centered task-reward signal. This separates reward centering from prompt-specific standard-deviation normalization.
\paragraph{Response budget.}
At fixed response count $B$, single-response sampling covers up to $B$ prompts; grouped sampling covers $B/k$. Larger groups trade prompt coverage for exploration of each prompt. Runtime and memory also depend on response length, batching, optimizer state, and rollout implementation.
\paragraph{Evaluation and attribution.}
The tool-use metric average@32 averages success across 32 sampled attempts; it differs from pass@32, which counts success if at least one attempt succeeds. The reported tool-use mean is an unweighted average across five benchmarks. The improvements of 1.52 and 2.25 percentage points over GRPO and PPO are measured for the complete training configuration. The evaluated configuration combines group-mean reward centering, global advantage normalization, a separate KL surrogate, and response-wise loss averaging.
